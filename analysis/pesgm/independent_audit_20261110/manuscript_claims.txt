5: Abstract—Power system planners use capacity expansion models to decide which power plants, storage systems, and transmission lines to build. ReEDS can answer these questions, but each run takes time. We test a faster approach: a surrogate model that learns from completed ReEDS runs. We built a prototype for the ERCOT power system in Texas. We ran all 486 combinations of six scenario choices and used the results to train seven machine-learning methods. The models estimate 66 system-wide and 308 regional results for 2050. In 10-fold cross-validation, Random Forest reached mean (median) R² of 0.75 (0.88) for system-wide results and 0.63 (0.72) for regional results. For total cost, transmission, and major technologies, R² was about 0.9 or higher. Four correction rules removed cost-accounting errors and reduced mismatches between regional and system totals by 92%, with little change in accuracy. The weakest results were for quantities that barely change and technologies that are rarely built. The dashboard also shows a 90% band for each prediction, which covers the true value about 90% of the time in cross-validation. Predictions take seconds, compared with 37–63 minutes per ReEDS run. This first test shows promise and identifies what remains to be checked before moving to the national model.

62: We test seven common methods: ridge, lasso, k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), Random Forest [14], XGBoost [15], NGBoost [16], and a neural network (MLP). Five inputs are coded as 0, 1, or 2, and the policy input as 0 or 1. We train a separate model for each output. Any scaling uses only the training cases in each fold. We keep model settings fixed rather than choosing them based on test results. Random Forest uses 200 trees and at least two samples per leaf; XGBoost uses 200 trees, depth 6, a learning rate of 0.1, and no monotone constraints.

63: We use 10-fold cross-validation to test predictions on cases the model has not seen. We divide the 486 cases into ten groups. Each model trains on nine groups and predicts the remaining group. We repeat this until every case has a prediction from a model that did not train on it. These are called out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. The test cases combine already sampled scenario levels in new ways. This does not test values between those levels, cases outside the design, or the national model.

64: We report R² for outputs that change across cases. An R² of 1 is perfect; 0 is no better than predicting the average. For uncertainty, each prediction comes with a 90% band: the prediction plus or minus the 90th percentile of that output’s absolute OOF error, following split conformal prediction [17]. The method works with any of the tested models. In a cross-fold check, the bands cover the true value about 90% of the time, both before and after the correction rules.

162: The correction rules substantially improve the consistency of the predictions. Before correction, predicted total cost differed by more than 1% from the sum of its components in 355 of the 486 cases. After correction, the two values agree exactly. Regional results also frequently disagreed with the predicted system total: 64% of the 23,544 eligible comparisons had errors above 1%. The corrections reduce these mismatches by 92.2%, leaving 1,167 comparisons for which regional rescaling is not applied.

166: Users can explore the six scenario choices and view system-wide or regional predictions in a browser dashboard (Fig. 6). Each prediction comes with a 90% band, and a second view colors each bar by the width of its band relative to its value, so the user sees at a glance which numbers are tight and which are not. In a cross-fold check, the bands cover the true value about 90% of the time, both before and after the correction rules. A prediction takes a few seconds for system-wide results and about 10 to 15 s for regional results on a laptop, compared with 37 to 63 minutes for a ReEDS run, a speedup of roughly a thousand times.

177: This ERCOT study shows that a surrogate trained on 486 ReEDS runs and six scenario choices can reproduce many important planning results. Random Forest predicts major system costs, transmission, and technology outcomes with R² around 0.9 or higher, and simple corrections improve agreement among related outputs. The 90% bands cover the true value about 90% of the time in cross-validation. Predictions are less reliable for quantities that change very little or for technologies built in only a few cases. Future work will test intermediate scenarios, more efficient training designs, and joint constraints before extending the approach to the national model.
